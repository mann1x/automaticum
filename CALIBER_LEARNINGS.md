- **[gotcha]** `automaticum run start` takes one `-task` per batch, so a pool over N tasks is N batches queued one after another on the lane, not interleaved; reordering the loop sets the queue order. Only a campaign `[[batch]]` (`tasks = [...]`, `runs = n`) interleaves across tasks, and campaigns have no `capture_requests` yet. A batch whose runs are training material therefore uses the `run start` loop with `-capture-requests`. CLI flags go before positionals.
- **[gotcha]** Judge a loop run from the whole wire, not its first requests: count reasoning chars per turn. In `<data_root>/runs/<id>/out/wire.jsonl` a chat row's `response_body` is the raw SSE stream. Keep the `data: {` lines, `json.loads` each, concatenate `choices[].delta.reasoning_content`, `.content` and `.tool_calls` per request, and take the last `finish_reason` (`None` on a cut turn). A tools-less request with `reasoning_budget_tokens: 0` and `finish_reason: stop` is the SDK's forced rewrite after a cut, not the arm's setting. While the run is in flight, the runner's trips are in the lane container's stdout: `docker logs <container> | grep '"note":"guard_trip"'` gives rows with `guard`, `phase`, `cause`, `iteration` and `message`. A bounded `timeout 15 ./bin/automaticum run tail <id> > <work>/tail.txt` carries the same rows from the moment it attached.
- **[gotcha]** The runner's `guard_trip` note keeps only `guard`, `phase`, `cause`, `iteration` and `message`. The guard's exact judgement is only in the SDK's own `notice` event, read with `automaticum logs -json <id> | grep reasoning_loop`. Its `metadata.verdict` is `{kind (e.g. line-cycle), period, cycles, chars, sample}`, plus `consecutiveTrips` and `channel`. The response the guard cut is the capture's `*.response.json` with `complete: false` just before the notice's timestamp, and its reasoning length equals `verdict.chars` exactly. Use these two together to locate a cut span without re-running a detector.

- **[pattern]** A run's wall and idle limits are the lower of the arm's and the task's, so an arm can never lengthen a task's cap.
